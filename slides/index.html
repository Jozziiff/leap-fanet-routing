Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
A flying ad hoc network, a FANET, is different from a ground network in three ways. [0] There is no infrastructure: each drone relays packets for the others. [1] The drones move fast, in three dimensions. [2] So links appear and disappear all the time, often while a packet is on its way.

Drones move.Links break.

1A forwards packets to B over one radio link.

2B drifts towards the edge of A's range.

3Out of range: the frame is lost, and the MAC keeps retrying a neighbour that has left.

Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
[1] Here is the basic event. A forwards packets to B. [2] B drifts towards the edge of A's range. [3] Now B is out of range: the frame is lost, and the MAC retries the same neighbour five times before giving up. Most protocols react only after this, so every break costs a burst of lost packets and a delay while the route is repaired.

Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
The simulator ships with four families of protocols. First, topology-based. [0] DSDV keeps a route to every destination, refreshed by periodic updates, so a route is always ready; but between two updates the table describes where the drones were, not where they are. [1] GRAd forwards by broadcast: any neighbour closer to the destination can relay. That is robust, but there are no acknowledgements, so a lost frame is never retried.

Greedy: local,simple, blind.

Greedy

position-based · the base of our protocol

Good atNeeds only its neighbours' positions, carried by hello messages.

Good atDecides per packet, at every hop: no route to build or repair.

Fails whenIt chooses by distance alone, never asking how long the link will last.

Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
Second, position-based routing, with Greedy. [0] Each drone knows its neighbours' positions from their hello messages, and sends the packet to the neighbour closest to the destination. No routes, no tables to repair: a fresh decision at every hop. That is why we chose it as the base of our protocol. [1] Its weakness: it picks by distance only, and never asks how long that link will last.

Predict with an oracle,or learn from losses.

OPAR

prediction · centralised

Good atPlans paths around predicted link lifetimes.

Fails whenIt reads every drone's true position: an oracle no real drone has.

Q-Routing · QGeo · Q-FANET

learning

Good atLearn which neighbours deliver, with no model of the radio.

Fails whenThey learn from outcomes: a packet must be lost before they adapt.

Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
Third and fourth, prediction and learning. [0] OPAR does predict link lifetimes and plans around them. But in the simulator it is centralised: the source reads the true position of every drone. It is a useful upper reference, not something a real drone could run. [1] The Q-learning protocols learn which neighbours deliver. They need no model, but they learn from outcomes: packets have to be lost before they adapt.

[4] The blind spot: predicting a break with only what a drone really receives, its neighbours' hello messages. But first, we had to measure whether breaks really matter.

Every lost frame,classified.

1We log every transmission attempt with the real distance between the two drones.

2Receiver in range, frame lost: collision.

3Receiver out of range: link break.

7 protocols · 11 configurations · 3 seeds
We instrumented the simulator without changing its behaviour. [1] Every transmission attempt is logged with the true distance between the two drones. [2] Receiver in range but frame lost: something interfered, a collision. [3] Receiver out of range: a link break. The simulator's radio has a hard range, so this split is exact.

Collisions are absorbed.Link breaks are not.

99–100%

of the losses that are link breaks or collisions are link breaks, for every unicast protocol

≤ 0.09%

of generated packets end as collisions at the reference point, unicast protocols

7 protocols · 11 configurations · 3 seeds
[0] What we understood: a collision is temporary, the MAC retries and the next attempt usually gets through. A link break is permanent: every retry goes to a drone that is gone. [1] So for every unicast protocol, 99 to 100 percent of the losses that are link breaks or collisions are link breaks. [2] Collisions are nearly fully absorbed: at our reference point, at most 0.09 percent of packets. The problem is mobility, not the MAC.

Greedy picksthe edge.

1Greedy forwards to the neighbour closest to the destination, which sits near the edge of range.

2That neighbour flies away. Its table entry lives 2 s, so Greedy still sees it.

3Five attempts go to a neighbour that has already left.

9.8 %of Greedy's packets are lost to link breaks at 10 m/s, and 16.0 % at 20 m/s.
Greedy · N = 30 · λ = 1 pkt/s per drone · 3 seeds
Why does Greedy suffer most? [1] The neighbour closest to the destination is usually the farthest from us, at the edge of range. [2] That is exactly the one most likely to leave, and the table keeps it for two seconds after its last hello. [3] So all five attempts go to a drone that already left: about one packet in ten at 10 metres per second, one in six at 20.

How long willthis link last?

1Each hello now carries the drone's velocity, next to its position.

2The receiver extrapolates where that neighbour is now.

3Distance, relative speed and radio range give the time until they separate: the lifetime τ̂.

The 3-D link expiration time · +128 bits per hello
Our protocol is called LEAP-GR, link-expiration-aware predictive greedy routing. Its core is one estimate. [0] Each hello already carries the drone's position; we add its velocity. [1] When a drone receives it, it extrapolates where the neighbour is now. [2] From the distance, the relative velocity and the radio range, a closed formula gives the time until the two drones separate: the link lifetime, tau hat. We use 90 percent of the range as a safety margin.

Greedy, unless the linkis about to break.

1Take Greedy's choice.

2Its link will last? Keep it.

3About to break (τ̂ < τth)? Switch to the best neighbour whose link will last.

4No such neighbour? Keep Greedy's choice: never worse than Greedy.

τth = 0.25 s, chosen on separate seeds by a rule fixed in advance
Then the rule, which fits in one sentence: behave like Greedy, unless the link is about to break. [0] Start from Greedy's choice. [1] If its link will last, keep it. [2] If its lifetime is under the threshold, switch to the best neighbour that still makes progress and whose link will last. [3] If there is none, keep Greedy's choice: by construction, it is never worse than Greedy. The threshold, a quarter of a second, was chosen on separate seeds before the final tests.

The MAC staysthe same.

1On a missing ACK, the MAC asks the routing layer through the platform's penalize hook.

2LEAP-GR redirects the retries only if the current hop is predicted to break.

3A reactive control redirects after every failure: it tells us what prediction adds.

A new module in routing/ · next_hop_selection, packet_reception, penalize
One constraint from the subject: the MAC must be identical in every comparison. [0] So we repair through a hook the platform already has: when an ACK is missing, the MAC calls the routing layer. [1] LEAP-GR uses it to redirect the retries, only if the current hop is predicted to break. [2] We also built a control that redirects after every failure, without prediction. Comparing the two isolates what prediction itself adds.

What we learned from the event logs: almost every switch skips a neighbour that, by its own last hello, has already left. So the gain is not long-range fortune-telling. It is refusing to trust a stale table entry, exactly Greedy's failure from before.

Where it stopshelping.

Heavy loadWhen the channel saturates, packets die in queues, not on broken links: reacting does better.

Slow swarmWhen drones move slowly, breaks are rare and there is little to predict.

ScopeSimulation only, open 3-D space, 3 seeds per point.

BonusCross-layer weighting by MAC retries: fewer ACK timeouts under heavy load, same delivery.

Youssef Hamdani · Rayen Hamza
It has limits, and they make sense. Under heavy load packets die in queues, not on broken links, so prediction cannot help and reacting does better. In a slow swarm breaks are rare. And this is simulation only, with three seeds per point. [1] As a bonus, we weighted the choice by the MAC retry rate: it reduces ACK timeouts under heavy load, but delivery does not change.
